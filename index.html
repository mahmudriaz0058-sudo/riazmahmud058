About

Concrete is the most widely used material after water, and every tonne of cement carries a carbon cost. My work starts from one question: how can waste become concrete that is stronger, more durable and lower in carbon, and how can we predict its performance before it is cast?

I am a Building Engineering and Construction Management graduate of KUET (4th in department, Dean’s List). I approach that question with three tools: laboratory experimentation on high-strength and low-carbon concretes, microstructural diagnosis that explains why a mix performs as it does (SEM, EDS, XRD, FTIR), and physics-informed, explainable machine learning that turns test data into reliable predictions. I have also worked on construction-safety research combining survey analytics, explainable AI and BIM–VR training.

My next step is graduate research (MS or PhD) that advances sustainable, durable concrete, building on my experimental and data-driven work in areas such as durability and service-life prediction, low-carbon materials, data-driven mix design and structural health monitoring.

RESEARCH INTERESTS

I study how waste-derived materials can make high-strength concrete sustainable without sacrificing performance, and how physics and data can explain and predict that performance.

  • Sustainable high-strength concreteRecycling construction and demolition waste and industrial by-products as aggregates and supplementary binders, and the mechanisms by which nano-additives densify the cement matrix and interfacial transition zone.
  • Durability and microstructureChloride transport, sorptivity and non-destructive evaluation, linked to SEM, EDS, XRD and FTIR evidence; service-life prediction under extreme conditions.
  • Physics-informed, explainable machine learningEmbedding the gel-space ratio and water-to-binder ratio in strength models, interpreting them with SHAP, and transferring them to new recycled aggregates with little local data.
  • Low-carbon binders and life-cycle assessmentGeopolymer, bacteria-induced recycled and polymer-modified concretes, weighed on strength, cost, fire resistance and embodied carbon.

Next: extending this work toward durability and service-life prediction, data-driven design of low-carbon concrete, and the link between material performance and structural health monitoring.

Upcycling Broken Pile Head Concrete as Coarse Aggregate in Nano-silica Modified High-Strength Concrete with Waste Glass Powder: Densification of the Matrix and ITZ

Mahmud, S. R. (first author), Sobuz, M. H. R. (corresponding author), et al.

Experimental study · B.Sc. thesis research, KUET

Read abstract

Large-scale cement production and mining of natural coarse aggregate (NCA) generate considerable pressure on the environment, while wasted glass and demolished pile heads remain underutilized resources. Broken pile head aggregate (BPHA), mortar-coated concrete debris routinely discarded during pile foundation construction, is structurally comparable to recycled coarse aggregate, yet is largely absent from the literature. This research investigates the joint use of BPHA and waste glass powder (GP) in high-strength concrete under a constant dosage of nano-silica (NS). Five mixes were prepared by replacing NCA with 0–60% BPHA and cement with 0–10% GP at equal increments, with NS fixed at 2% of cement (OPC). Slump, Kelly ball penetration and fresh density were evaluated, while compressive strength, splitting tensile strength and rebound number were determined at 7, 28 and 56 days. Microstructural (SEM and EDS) and sustainability (embodied CO2 and energy) properties were also assessed. The results showed that workability and fresh density decreased with increasing GP and BPHA content. The 2.5% GP + 15% BPHA mix achieved the highest compressive and splitting tensile strengths, with gains of 9.1% and 3.4% at 28 days over the control, owing to the secondary C–S–H formed by the pozzolanic reaction of GP and NS. SEM revealed a denser interfacial transition zone (ITZ) and EDS revealed a lower Ca/Si ratio (1.84 to 1.58) in this mix, while embodied CO2 and energy decreased by up to 9.50% and 8.40%, respectively. This study identifies a large neglected stream of construction waste and shows that GP and NS densify the matrix and ITZ around BPHA making it suitable for sustainable high-strength concrete.

Physics-Informed Explainable Machine Learning for Compressive Strength of Glass Powder–Nano-Silica Recycled Aggregate Concrete: A Broken Pile Head Case Study

Mahmud, S. R. (first author), Sobuz, M. H. R. (corresponding author), et al.

Experimental data + machine learning

0.915Best testing R² among eight tree-based models

Read abstract

Cement production and natural aggregate extraction raise CO2 emissions and deplete resources, while waste glass and waste concrete are largely landfilled. This study combined waste glass powder (GP) as a partial cement replacement, broken pile head aggregate (BPHA) as recycled coarse aggregate and nano-silica (NS), and developed a physics-informed explainable machine learning (ML) framework to predict its compressive strength (CS). Five mixes with 0–10% GP, 0–60% BPHA with 2% fixed NS were tested at 7, 28 and 56 days and combined with 491 literature records into a database of 506 records, in which the water-to-binder ratio and the Powers gel-space ratio were added as physics descriptors. Eight single and hybrid tree-based models were compared by 5-fold cross-validation and a 20% hold-out test. The physics descriptors were assessed by an ablation test and a physics-guided dual-branch neural network, and the selected model was interpreted by SHAP and PDP analyses. The mix with 2.5% GP and 15% BPHA achieved the highest 28-day CS of 50.75 MPa, 9.1% above the control, owing to secondary C–S–H formation by GP and NS, while 10% GP with 60% BPHA retained 97% of the control strength. Boosting models reached testing R² of 0.894–0.915, and the gel-space ratio was the most influential input. A literature-only model failed to predict the BPHA mixes (R² = −0.51), whereas adding four local mixes reduced the MAPE to 3.2–4.9%. GP–NS–BPHA concrete is therefore a viable sustainable material, and physics-informed ML can support its mix design once a few local mixes are tested.

UNDERGRADUATE THESIS · AUGUST 2026

Thesis at a glance

In short: two construction wastes — waste glass powder and broken pile head concrete — can replace part of the cement and coarse aggregate in high-strength concrete. The best balance came from 2.5% glass powder + 15% pile head aggregate, which was stronger, less permeable and cheaper than the control.

Objectives

  • Develop a high-strength concrete that reuses waste glass powder (GP) as partial cement and broken pile head aggregate (BPHA) as recycled coarse aggregate, with 2% nano-silica.
  • Evaluate its fresh, mechanical, durability and microstructural properties and identify the optimum GP–BPHA combination.
  • Compare embodied CO2, embodied energy and cost with conventional high-strength concrete.

Methods

  • Materials: waste glass ground to <75 µm powder; pile heads collected from site, crushed and graded into BPHA.
  • Mix design: ACI 211.4R-08, w/b 0.30; five mixes from control to 10% GP + 60% BPHA, nano-silica fixed at 2%.
  • Fresh tests: slump, compacting factor, Kelly ball penetration and density.
  • Hardened tests (7, 28, 56 days): compressive and splitting tensile strength, rebound hammer, UPV, RCPT and sorptivity.
  • Microstructure and sustainability: XRD, SEM, EDS, FTIR, life cycle assessment and cost analysis.

Key Results

  • 2.5% GP + 15% BPHA was the optimum mix for strength, durability and microstructure.
  • It reached 53.25 MPa compressive and 4.20 MPa tensile strength at 56 days, 4.72% and 2.94% above the control.
  • It showed the lowest chloride permeability (~1000 C) and the lowest water sorptivity.
  • EDS showed a lower Ca/Si ratio (1.84 → 1.58) and SEM a denser ITZ, explaining the gains.
  • Higher replacement cut material cost by up to ~23% and lowered embodied CO2, but reduced workability and strength.
